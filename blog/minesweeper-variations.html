Minesweeper Variations: 7 Types of Minesweeper You Should Try

The classic grid is just the beginning. Here are seven ways minesweeper has evolved.

August 23, 2026 · 9 min read · Guide

When most people think of minesweeper, they picture the gray grid from Windows: square cells, numbers 1–8, right-click to flag. That's the classic version, and it's been the standard for over 30 years. But minesweeper as a concept is far more flexible than that — developers have been remixing the formula for decades, changing board shapes, adding scoring systems, and even making it multiplayer.

Here are seven distinct variations of minesweeper, what makes each one unique, and where to try them.

1. Classic Minesweeper

The original. A rectangular grid of covered cells, each hiding either a number or a mine. Numbers tell you how many mines are in the 8 adjacent cells. Flag mines, reveal safe cells, clear the board. That's it.

Classic minesweeper is the version that shipped with Windows 3.1 in 1992 and has been ported to every platform since. Its brilliance is in its simplicity: the rules take two minutes to learn, but the deduction depth is essentially infinite. Every board is a new puzzle, and there's always a faster, more elegant solution.

Bean Boom's Classic Mode is a faithful implementation of this variant, with three standard difficulties (9×9, 16×16, 16×30) and time-based leaderboards. New to the game? Start with our beginner guide.

2. Bean Boom Egg Mode (Scoring Minesweeper)

Egg Mode is a scoring variation of classic minesweeper. The board and rules are identical, but every action earns points, and correct flagging triggers Bean Boom chain explosions that cascade across the board.

What makes Egg Mode distinct from other scoring variants is the combo system: chain actions within a 3-second window to build a multiplier, and hit a streak of 20+ to enter FEVER mode with a 4× score cap. This turns minesweeper from a turn-based logic puzzle into something that feels like an arcade game — the same deduction skills, but with a heartbeat.

It's the only variation on this list that fundamentally changes how you play rather than just the board shape or rules. Flagging becomes offensive rather than defensive. Speed becomes as important as accuracy. And the leaderboard ranks by score with S/A/B/C tiers, not just by time.

3. Hexagonal Minesweeper

In hexagonal minesweeper, the square grid is replaced by a honeycomb of hexagonal cells. Each cell has 6 neighbors instead of 8 (no diagonals — every adjacent cell shares an edge). The numbers still indicate mine count among neighbors, but the deduction feels different because the geometry is different.

Hex minesweeper is popular among players who have mastered the square grid and want a fresh challenge. The deduction patterns you memorized for square grids (1-2-1, 1-2-2-1, etc.) don't apply — you have to relearn the logic from scratch. It's like learning minesweeper again, which is either exhilarating or exhausting depending on your temperament.

Where to try: Several browser hex minesweeper implementations exist. Bean Boom is currently square-grid only, but it's on our radar.

4. Multiplayer Minesweeper

Multiplayer minesweeper puts multiple players on the same board simultaneously, each racing to claim territory. The first player to flag a mine gets credit for it. The first to clear their section wins. Some versions also let you "steal" territory from opponents.

The interesting strategic layer is that you can see other players' progress, which means you can infer information from where they're flagging and where they're avoiding. It's minesweeper meets real-time strategy.

The downside is that the real-time element adds pressure that can undermine the careful, methodical thinking that makes minesweeper satisfying. It's a different experience — fun, but not a replacement for solo play.

5. Minesweeper with Lives

In this variation, hitting a mine doesn't end the game — it costs you one of a limited number of lives (usually 3). You can keep playing as long as you have lives left, and the mine you hit is revealed so you know where it was.

This is a great variation for beginners and casual players who find the instant game-over of classic minesweeper too punishing. It lets you learn from mistakes without restarting from scratch. The trade-off is that the stakes are lower, which reduces the tension that makes classic minesweeper thrilling.

Some implementations also add a score penalty for each life lost, which preserves some of the tension while being more forgiving.

6. No-Guess Minesweeper

One of the most frustrating moments in classic minesweeper is the forced guess — a board state where logic alone cannot determine which of two cells is a mine, and you must flip a coin. This happens on roughly 5% of Expert boards.

No-guess minesweeper generates boards that are guaranteed solvable through pure logic. The generator ensures that at every point in the game, there's always at least one cell whose status can be deduced with certainty from the visible numbers. No coin flips, ever.

This variation is beloved by purists who believe minesweeper should be a pure logic puzzle, not a game that sometimes demands luck. Competitive minesweeper communities often separate "no-guess" records from "anything-goes" records for this reason.

Bean Boom's board generator includes first-click safety (your first reveal is never a mine or adjacent to one), though it doesn't guarantee full no-guess solvability. For most players on Easy and Medium, forced guesses are rare enough to not be a problem.

7. Themed Minesweeper

Themed minesweeper doesn't change the rules — it changes the visuals. Instead of gray cells and numbers, you might see jewels, candy, animals, or any other skin. The gameplay is identical to classic minesweeper.

This is the most superficial variation, but it has real appeal for casual players who want variety. A visually appealing theme can make the game feel fresh even when the logic is the same. Bean Boom uses a "bean" theme — the beads on the board look like colorful beans, and mines explode in a satisfying burst — which is why the chain reaction feature is called a "Bean Boom."

Which Variation Should You Try?

If you're new to minesweeper, start with classic (Bean Boom's Classic Mode) to learn the rules. Once you're comfortable, try Egg Mode for a scoring twist that changes how you think about flagging and speed. If you want to push further, look for hexagonal minesweeper for a geometry challenge, or no-guess minesweeper if you want guaranteed-solvable boards.

The beauty of minesweeper as a game concept is that it's simple enough to remix endlessly. Each variation keeps the core appeal — the satisfaction of logical deduction — while adding a new dimension. Find the one that fits your mood.
