Minesweeper Rules Explained

Every rule of minesweeper, including the ones tutorials usually skip.

August 22, 2026 · 7 min read · Reference

Most minesweeper tutorials teach you the two-minute version of the rules and leave it at that. This guide goes deeper: the full rule set, how boards are actually generated, what numbers can and cannot tell you, and the edge cases — forced guesses, chording, and win conditions — that confuse even experienced players.

Rule 1: The Objective

A minesweeper board is a grid of covered cells. A fixed number of mines are hidden among them. You win by revealing every cell that does not contain a mine. You lose the moment you reveal a mine.

Note what the objective does not say: you do not need to locate every mine, and you do not need to flag anything. Flags are optional tooling, not a win condition. This distinction matters more than beginners realize — see our flagging guide for why some experts barely flag at all.

Mine density is why difficulty scales non-linearly: Hard has less than 5 empty cells per mine, so nearly every reveal carries information and risk.

How mines are actually placed

In modern implementations — Bean Boom included — the mines are placed after your first click. The generator first takes your click position, then distributes the mines among the remaining cells, guaranteeing:

  1. Your first click never hits a mine.
  2. Your first click isn't even adjacent to a mine (in Bean Boom), so you always start with an opening rather than an isolated number.

This is why the "start anywhere" advice is safe: there is no strategy for the first click because the board adapts to it.

Rule 3: The Numbers

When you reveal a safe cell, it displays the count of mines in its eight adjacent cells (orthogonal and diagonal). That's the entire information model of the game:

  • 1–8: that many adjacent mines.
  • Blank: zero adjacent mines. The game automatically flood-reveals the connected blank region and the numbered ring around it.

Two corollaries follow directly from this rule, and they generate every deduction in the game:

The two fundamental deductions

Rule of saturation: if a number's count equals its count of covered neighbors, all those neighbors are mines.

Rule of satisfaction: if a number's count equals the flags already around it, every other covered neighbor is safe.

Everything else — 1-2-1 patterns, subtraction logic, set reasoning — is these two rules composed together. We cover that machinery in reading the numbers and the pattern library.

Rule 4: Flags and Question Marks

Right-click (desktop) or long-press (mobile) cycles a cell between covered, flagged, and — in some implementations — question mark.

  • Flag: marks a suspected mine. Blocks accidental reveals; required for chording.
  • Question mark: an optional uncertainty marker. Bean Boom omits it — serious players find it slows them down without adding information.

One warning that belongs in every rules discussion: flags can be wrong. The game never verifies your flags. A wrong flag silently corrupts every later deduction that counts it, which is why disciplined players flag only on certainty.

Rule 5: Chording (the Double-Click Rule)

Chording is the least-known rule that changes everything about playing efficiently. If the flags around a revealed number already satisfy that number — a "2" with two flags around it — you can activate the number itself (double-click on desktop, double-tap on mobile in Bean Boom) to reveal all its other covered neighbors at once.

Because chording fires up to seven reveals in one action, it's the single biggest source of speed for fast players. And because it only works on satisfied numbers, it's safe — if your flags are correct. Chord on a wrong flag and you will detonate a mine. Chording is a trust accelerator: it makes good flagging faster and bad flagging fatal.

Rule 6: Winning and Losing

  • Win: the last safe cell is revealed. In Bean Boom's Classic Mode your time is recorded; fast wins land on the time leaderboard.
  • Loss: any mine is revealed. The board then reveals all remaining mines so you can audit your mistakes.

There is no score partial credit in classic minesweeper — a 95%-solved board loses exactly as hard as an untouched one. (Bean Boom's Egg Mode is different: it's a scoring game where chains and combos matter, and losing still records what you earned. See the Egg Mode page.)

Rule 7: Guessing Is Legal (and Sometimes Forced)

Purists dislike this, but it's part of the game: some boards reach states where no safe cell can be deduced. The classic example is an untouched 50/50 corner late in a Hard game. When logic dead-ends, you must guess — the rules permit it, the game requires it.

Good guessing discipline:

  1. Re-scan the entire board first. "No logical move" is wrong more often than it's right.
  2. Choose guesses that yield maximum information whether you live or die — a guess inside a dense number region, not an isolated corner.
  3. Prefer low-probability-mine areas: a 1-in-4 guess beats a 1-in-2 if you get to pick.
  4. Guess early rather than late when a board is visibly unsolvable — fail fast, restart fast.

Edge Cases Worth Knowing

  • The mine counter can go negative if you over-flag. It's counting flags, not truth.
  • A number can be at most 8, and an 8 is vanishingly rare — all eight neighbors mined.
  • Walls and corners shrink the neighbor set: a corner cell has only 3 neighbors, so a corner "1" means the mine is among those 3 — strong information precisely where players forget to look.
  • Flags don't protect you from yourself in every client — but in Bean Boom a flagged cell cannot be revealed, period.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to flag all the mines to win?

No. The win condition is revealing all safe cells. Many strong players flag sparsely and win constantly.

Is the first click really always safe?

Yes — in Bean Boom and all modern implementations, the board is generated after the first click. Your opening reveal can never be a mine.

Can a minesweeper board be impossible?

Rarely, a board reaches a state where a guess is mathematically forced (like a 50/50 corner). It's not "impossible," but it's not fully solvable by logic either. It's an accepted quirk of the game.

What happens if I flag a cell that isn't a mine?

Nothing immediately — the game doesn't check flags. But your deductions that count that flag become wrong, and chording near it can detonate a real mine. Wrong flags are landmines for your own logic.

Are Bean Boom's rules different from classic minesweeper?

Classic Mode follows all the rules on this page exactly. Egg Mode keeps the same rules and layers a scoring system on top — correct flags trigger chain blasts that reveal territory and feed combos.
